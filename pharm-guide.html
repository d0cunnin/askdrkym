Pharmacology Drug Class Master Guide

For Nursing Students — Consistent Format Across Every Category

Cardiovascular Medications
Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitors Suffix: -pril
Lisinopril, Enalapril, Ramipril
▼
Uses
Hypertension, heart failure, diabetic kidney protection
Mechanism of Action
Blocks conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II, causing vasodilation and reducing blood pressure
Side Effects
Dry cough, hyperkalemia, hypotension, angioedema
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; discontinue drug, treat angioedema with epinephrine if severe
Nursing Considerations
Monitor blood pressure, potassium levels, and creatinine (kidney function)
Patient Teaching
Report any facial or throat swelling immediately. Avoid potassium supplements or salt substitutes unless prescribed. Do not stop abruptly.
Angiotensin Receptor Blockers Suffix: -sartan
Losartan, Valsartan
▼
Uses
Hypertension, heart failure, diabetic kidney protection
Mechanism of Action
Blocks angiotensin II receptors directly, causing vasodilation — similar result to Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitors but without blocking the enzyme
Side Effects
Hyperkalemia, hypotension. Unlike Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitors, does NOT typically cause dry cough
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; discontinue drug, supportive care
Nursing Considerations
Monitor blood pressure and potassium. Do not use in pregnancy.
Patient Teaching
Avoid potassium supplements unless prescribed. Report dizziness or swelling. Do not stop abruptly.
Beta Blockers Suffix: -olol
Metoprolol, Atenolol, Propranolol
▼
Uses
Hypertension, angina, cardiac arrhythmias, heart failure
Mechanism of Action
Blocks beta-adrenergic receptors, slowing heart rate, reducing blood pressure, and decreasing cardiac workload
Side Effects
Bradycardia, hypotension, fatigue, bronchospasm (use with caution in asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease)
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; Glucagon may be used for severe toxicity
Nursing Considerations
Check heart rate and blood pressure before each dose. Hold if heart rate is below 60 beats per minute or blood pressure is critically low.
Patient Teaching
Never stop abruptly — tapering is required to avoid rebound hypertension or angina. Report dizziness, slow heartbeat, or shortness of breath.
Calcium Channel Blockers
Amlodipine, Diltiazem, Verapamil
▼
Uses
Hypertension, angina, certain cardiac arrhythmias
Mechanism of Action
Blocks calcium entry into cardiac and smooth muscle cells, causing vasodilation and slowing heart conduction
Side Effects
Hypotension, peripheral edema, constipation (especially Verapamil), flushing, headache
Antidote / Reversal
Calcium gluconate or calcium chloride for severe overdose; supportive care
Nursing Considerations
Monitor blood pressure and heart rate. Verapamil and Diltiazem slow the heart more than Amlodipine.
Patient Teaching
Rise slowly to prevent dizziness. Increase fiber and fluids to manage constipation. Report swelling in legs or feet.
Loop Diuretics
Furosemide (Lasix) — prototype
▼
Uses
Edema from heart failure, kidney or liver disease; hypertension; pulmonary edema
Mechanism of Action
Inhibits sodium and chloride reabsorption in the loop of Henle, producing potent diuresis
Side Effects
Hypokalemia, dehydration, hypotension, ototoxicity (hearing loss — especially at high intravenous doses)
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; replace potassium and fluids as needed
Nursing Considerations
Monitor potassium, daily weights, intake and output, and blood pressure. Administer in the morning to avoid sleep disruption.
Patient Teaching
Eat potassium-rich foods (bananas, oranges) unless otherwise restricted. Weigh yourself daily. Report ringing in the ears or sudden hearing changes.
Thiazide Diuretics
Hydrochlorothiazide — prototype; Chlorthalidone
▼
Uses
Hypertension, mild edema, kidney stones (calcium)
Mechanism of Action
Inhibits sodium reabsorption in the distal tubule of the kidney, promoting fluid excretion
Side Effects
Hypokalemia, hyperglycemia, hyperuricemia (gout), hypercalcemia, hyperlipidemia
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; replace electrolytes and fluids as needed
Nursing Considerations
Monitor potassium, glucose, and uric acid levels. Administer in the morning.
Patient Teaching
Eat potassium-rich foods. Monitor blood sugar if diabetic. Report joint pain (possible gout). Use sunscreen — increases sun sensitivity.
Potassium-Sparing Diuretics
Spironolactone (Aldactone) — prototype; Amiloride
▼
Uses
Heart failure, hypertension, edema; used with other diuretics to prevent potassium loss
Mechanism of Action
Blocks aldosterone receptors in the collecting duct, retaining potassium while excreting sodium and water
Side Effects
Hyperkalemia (most important), gynecomastia (breast enlargement in men) with Spironolactone, hypotension
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; treat hyperkalemia with calcium gluconate, sodium bicarbonate, or dialysis if severe
Nursing Considerations
Monitor potassium closely — do NOT give potassium supplements routinely. Monitor renal function.
Patient Teaching
Avoid potassium supplements and salt substitutes. Report irregular heartbeat or muscle weakness. Report breast tenderness or enlargement.
Nitrates
Nitroglycerin — prototype; Isosorbide dinitrate
▼
Uses
Acute angina, chest pain, heart failure, hypertensive emergency
Mechanism of Action
Converts to nitric oxide, relaxing smooth muscle in blood vessel walls and reducing cardiac preload
Side Effects
Headache (very common), hypotension, reflex tachycardia, dizziness, flushing
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; manage hypotension with intravenous fluids and positioning
Nursing Considerations
Sit or lie patient down before administering. Monitor blood pressure closely. Do not give if systolic blood pressure is below 90 mmHg. Never use with phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors (e.g., Sildenafil — causes severe hypotension).
Patient Teaching
Sit before taking sublingual dose. May repeat every 5 minutes up to 3 times — call 911 if not relieved. Store in original dark glass bottle. Headache is common and expected.
Statins (3-Hydroxy-3-Methylglutaryl Coenzyme A Reductase Inhibitors) Suffix: -statin
Atorvastatin, Simvastatin, Rosuvastatin
▼
Uses
High cholesterol, prevention of heart attack and stroke, cardiovascular disease risk reduction
Mechanism of Action
Inhibits 3-Hydroxy-3-Methylglutaryl Coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase enzyme in the liver, reducing cholesterol production and lowering low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels
Side Effects
Myopathy (muscle pain/weakness), rhabdomyolysis (rare but serious), elevated liver enzymes, headache
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; discontinue drug and provide supportive care for rhabdomyolysis
Nursing Considerations
Monitor liver function tests and creatine kinase levels. Be alert for signs of muscle breakdown.
Patient Teaching
Report muscle aches, weakness, or dark urine immediately. Avoid grapefruit juice with some statins (especially Simvastatin). Take in the evening for best effect with some statins.
Anticoagulants — Heparin (Unfractionated)
Heparin; Low Molecular Weight: Enoxaparin (Lovenox)
▼
Uses
Deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, acute coronary syndrome, prevention of clot formation
Mechanism of Action
Potentiates antithrombin III, which inactivates thrombin and factor Xa, preventing clot formation
Side Effects
Bleeding, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (a serious drop in platelets), injection site bruising
Antidote / Reversal
Protamine sulfate
Nursing Considerations
Monitor activated partial thromboplastin time (goal: 1.5–2.5 times normal). Monitor platelet count for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia. Assess for bleeding (urine, stool, gums).
Patient Teaching
Report any unusual bleeding or bruising. Use a soft toothbrush and electric razor. Avoid aspirin and other blood thinners unless prescribed.
Anticoagulants — Warfarin
Warfarin (Coumadin) — prototype
▼
Uses
Atrial fibrillation, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, mechanical heart valve protection
Mechanism of Action
Inhibits Vitamin K–dependent clotting factors (II, VII, IX, X), reducing the liver's ability to produce clotting proteins
Side Effects
Bleeding (major risk), bruising, hair loss, skin necrosis (rare)
Antidote / Reversal
Vitamin K (Phytonadione); fresh frozen plasma for urgent reversal
Nursing Considerations
Monitor International Normalized Ratio (goal: usually 2–3; 2.5–3.5 for mechanical valves). Many drug and food interactions. Consistent Vitamin K intake is key.
Patient Teaching
Keep Vitamin K intake consistent (do not suddenly increase or decrease leafy greens). Report bleeding. Get regular International Normalized Ratio blood tests. Avoid alcohol excess and over-the-counter pain relievers.
Direct Oral Anticoagulants
Apixaban (Eliquis), Rivaroxaban (Xarelto), Dabigatran (Pradaxa)
▼
Uses
Atrial fibrillation, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism prevention and treatment
Mechanism of Action
Directly inhibit specific clotting factors: Factor Xa (Apixaban, Rivaroxaban) or Thrombin/Factor IIa (Dabigatran)
Side Effects
Bleeding, bruising, gastrointestinal upset (especially Dabigatran)
Antidote / Reversal
Andexanet alfa (for Factor Xa inhibitors); Idarucizumab (Praxbind) for Dabigatran
Nursing Considerations
No routine International Normalized Ratio monitoring needed. Monitor renal function (drug is renally cleared). Assess for bleeding.
Patient Teaching
Do not stop without telling your provider. Take with food if gastrointestinal upset occurs (Dabigatran). Report unusual bleeding, blood in stool or urine.
Endocrine Medications
Insulin — Rapid-Acting
Lispro (Humalog), Aspart (NovoLog), Glulisine (Apidra)
▼
Uses
Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes; mealtime blood sugar control
Mechanism of Action
Replaces or supplements endogenous insulin; facilitates glucose uptake into cells and inhibits hepatic glucose production
Side Effects
Hypoglycemia (most critical), weight gain, injection site reactions, hypokalemia
Antidote / Reversal
Dextrose 50% intravenously or Glucagon intramuscularly for severe hypoglycemia
Nursing Considerations
Onset: 15 minutes — give with meals or immediately before. Monitor blood glucose before and after administration. Have hypoglycemia treatment available at bedside.
Patient Teaching
Inject 0–15 minutes before meals. Rotate injection sites. Recognize hypoglycemia signs (shakiness, sweating, confusion). Keep fast-acting sugar available (juice, glucose tablets).
Insulin — Short-Acting (Regular)
Regular Insulin (Humulin R, Novolin R)
▼
Uses
Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes; diabetic ketoacidosis (only insulin given by intravenous infusion)
Mechanism of Action
Replaces endogenous insulin; promotes cellular glucose uptake and reduces blood glucose levels
Side Effects
Hypoglycemia, hypokalemia, injection site reactions, weight gain
Antidote / Reversal
Dextrose 50% intravenously or Glucagon intramuscularly for severe hypoglycemia
Nursing Considerations
KEY POINT: Regular insulin is the ONLY insulin that can be given intravenously. Onset: 30–60 minutes subcutaneously. Give 30 minutes before meals.
Patient Teaching
Inject 30 minutes before meals. Monitor blood glucose regularly. Carry fast-acting sugar. Do not reuse needles.
Insulin — Intermediate-Acting (Neutral Protamine Hagedorn)
Neutral Protamine Hagedorn Insulin (Humulin N, Novolin N)
▼
Uses
Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes; basal insulin coverage over 12–16 hours
Mechanism of Action
Complexed with protamine to delay absorption; provides intermediate-duration glucose control
Side Effects
Hypoglycemia (peaks at 4–12 hours — watch for overnight lows), weight gain, injection site reactions
Antidote / Reversal
Dextrose 50% intravenously or Glucagon intramuscularly for severe hypoglycemia
Nursing Considerations
Appears cloudy — gently roll vial to mix; do NOT shake. Cannot be given intravenously. Peak action occurs at 4–12 hours post-injection.
Patient Teaching
Gently roll the vial before drawing up — do not shake. Monitor blood sugar overnight. Rotate injection sites consistently.
Insulin — Long-Acting (Basal)
Glargine (Lantus), Detemir (Levemir), Degludec (Tresiba)
▼
Uses
Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes; provides steady 24-hour basal insulin coverage
Mechanism of Action
Forms a precipitate at the injection site, releasing insulin slowly over 20–24 hours with no pronounced peak
Side Effects
Hypoglycemia (less common than rapid or intermediate insulins), weight gain, injection site reactions
Antidote / Reversal
Dextrose 50% intravenously or Glucagon intramuscularly for severe hypoglycemia
Nursing Considerations
Clear appearance — do NOT mix with other insulins. Give at the same time each day. Cannot be given intravenously. No pronounced peak to monitor for.
Patient Teaching
Give at the same time every day. Do not mix with other insulins in the same syringe. Store unopened pens/vials in the refrigerator; opened ones can stay at room temperature for 28–30 days.
Biguanides — Metformin
Metformin (Glucophage) — prototype
▼
Uses
Type 2 diabetes (first-line oral agent), polycystic ovary syndrome
Mechanism of Action
Reduces hepatic glucose production and improves insulin sensitivity; does not stimulate insulin release
Side Effects
Gastrointestinal upset (nausea, diarrhea, metallic taste), lactic acidosis (rare but serious), Vitamin B12 deficiency with long-term use
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; discontinue drug and provide supportive care; dialysis for severe lactic acidosis
Nursing Considerations
Hold 48 hours before and after intravenous contrast dye (risk of lactic acidosis). Monitor renal function — contraindicated with severe kidney impairment. Does NOT cause hypoglycemia when used alone.
Patient Teaching
Take with food to reduce stomach upset. Tell all providers you take Metformin before any imaging with contrast dye. Report muscle pain, weakness, or difficulty breathing (signs of lactic acidosis).
Sulfonylureas
Glipizide, Glyburide, Glimepiride
▼
Uses
Type 2 diabetes (oral agent when Metformin is not tolerated or insufficient)
Mechanism of Action
Stimulates insulin release from pancreatic beta cells regardless of blood glucose level
Side Effects
Hypoglycemia (most significant — drug stimulates insulin even when glucose is low), weight gain
Antidote / Reversal
Oral glucose for mild hypoglycemia; Dextrose 50% intravenously or Glucagon for severe hypoglycemia
Nursing Considerations
High risk for hypoglycemia — monitor blood glucose. Use with caution in elderly and those with kidney or liver disease. Ensure patient eats meals on schedule.
Patient Teaching
Take with meals. Do not skip meals. Carry a fast-acting sugar source. Avoid alcohol (increases hypoglycemia risk). Report signs of low blood sugar.
Thyroid Hormone Replacement
Levothyroxine (Synthroid, Levoxyl) — prototype
▼
Uses
Hypothyroidism (underactive thyroid), thyroid cancer suppression
Mechanism of Action
Replaces T4 (thyroxine), which is converted to active T3 in the body; restores normal metabolic rate
Side Effects
Signs of overdose (hyperthyroidism): tachycardia, palpitations, weight loss, heat intolerance, anxiety, insomnia
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; reduce dose; beta blockers can treat symptomatic tachycardia from overdose
Nursing Considerations
Monitor thyroid-stimulating hormone and T4 levels. Assess heart rate and blood pressure. Many drugs reduce absorption (calcium, iron, antacids).
Patient Teaching
Take every morning on an empty stomach, 30–60 minutes before food. Take at the same time each day. Do not take within 4 hours of calcium or iron supplements. Therapy is usually lifelong.
Corticosteroids (Glucocorticoids)
Prednisone, Methylprednisolone (Solu-Medrol), Dexamethasone, Hydrocortisone
▼
Uses
Inflammation, autoimmune conditions, allergic reactions, asthma exacerbations, organ transplant rejection prevention, adrenal insufficiency
Mechanism of Action
Suppresses the immune system and inhibits inflammatory mediators; mimics the body's natural cortisol
Side Effects
Hyperglycemia, increased infection risk, osteoporosis, weight gain, fluid retention, mood changes, adrenal suppression, gastric ulcers, cataracts with long-term use
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; must taper dose gradually to prevent adrenal crisis
Nursing Considerations
Monitor blood glucose closely (causes hyperglycemia). Monitor for signs of infection — drug masks fever. Administer with food. Never stop abruptly.
Patient Teaching
Never stop abruptly — must taper. Take with food to prevent stomach upset. Report signs of infection. If on long-term therapy: increase calcium and Vitamin D intake, use a fall-prevention plan.
Respiratory Medications
Short-Acting Beta Agonists (Rescue Inhalers)
Albuterol (ProAir, Ventolin) — prototype; Levalbuterol
▼
Uses
Acute bronchospasm, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbations; rescue inhaler
Mechanism of Action
Stimulates beta-2 adrenergic receptors in the bronchioles, causing bronchodilation (relaxing airway smooth muscle)
Side Effects
Tachycardia, tremor, nervousness, hypokalemia (with frequent use), palpitations
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; beta blockers can reverse effects but are generally contraindicated in asthma
Nursing Considerations
Monitor heart rate and respiratory status. If using multiple doses without relief, seek emergency care. Increasing frequency of use suggests worsening disease.
Patient Teaching
Use as a rescue inhaler for sudden symptoms. Shake before use. If using a spacer, use it consistently. Report if you are using the inhaler more than twice per week — this signals poor disease control.
Inhaled Corticosteroids
Fluticasone (Flovent), Budesonide (Pulmicort), Beclomethasone
▼
Uses
Long-term prevention and control of asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; NOT for acute attacks
Mechanism of Action
Reduces airway inflammation by suppressing inflammatory mediators locally in the lungs
Side Effects
Oral candidiasis (thrush), hoarseness, dysphonia. Systemic effects minimal with inhaled use.
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; for oral thrush, treat with Nystatin
Nursing Considerations
These are maintenance (controller) medications — they are NOT rescue inhalers. Monitor for oral candidiasis. Inspect mouth at each visit.
Patient Teaching
Rinse mouth and gargle with water after every use to prevent thrush. This is a daily preventive medication, not for sudden attacks — continue even when feeling well.
Inhaled Anticholinergics (Bronchodilators)
Ipratropium (Atrovent), Tiotropium (Spiriva)
▼
Uses
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (first-line), asthma (adjunct), bronchospasm
Mechanism of Action
Blocks muscarinic receptors in the airways, preventing bronchoconstriction and reducing mucus secretion
Side Effects
Dry mouth, urinary retention, constipation, blurred vision, tachycardia, paradoxical bronchospasm (rare)
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; Physostigmine for severe systemic anticholinergic toxicity
Nursing Considerations
Use with caution in patients with glaucoma or enlarged prostate. Monitor respiratory rate and breath sounds. Tiotropium is once daily; Ipratropium is multiple times daily.
Patient Teaching
This is a maintenance inhaler, not a rescue inhaler. Report urinary difficulty, eye pain, or worsening breathing. Sip water frequently for dry mouth.
Gastrointestinal Medications
Proton Pump Inhibitors Suffix: -prazole
Omeprazole (Prilosec), Pantoprazole (Protonix), Esomeprazole (Nexium)
▼
Uses
Gastroesophageal reflux disease, peptic ulcer disease, Zollinger-Ellison syndrome, prevention of stress ulcers in hospitalized patients
Mechanism of Action
Irreversibly binds to and inhibits the hydrogen/potassium adenosine triphosphatase pump (proton pump) in gastric parietal cells, blocking acid secretion
Side Effects
Headache, gastrointestinal upset, hypomagnesemia (with long-term use), Clostridium difficile infection risk, decreased calcium absorption, increased fracture risk
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; discontinue drug and manage symptoms
Nursing Considerations
Administer 30–60 minutes before the first meal of the day for best efficacy. Monitor magnesium levels with long-term use. Reassess need for continued therapy regularly.
Patient Teaching
Take 30–60 minutes before eating. Do not crush or chew delayed-release capsules. Report persistent diarrhea (possible Clostridium difficile). Long-term use increases bone fracture risk — maintain calcium and Vitamin D intake.
Histamine-2 Receptor Blockers (H2 Blockers)
Famotidine (Pepcid), Cimetidine
▼
Uses
Gastroesophageal reflux disease, peptic ulcer disease, heartburn, Zollinger-Ellison syndrome
Mechanism of Action
Blocks histamine-2 receptors on gastric parietal cells, reducing acid secretion (less potent than Proton Pump Inhibitors)
Side Effects
Headache, dizziness, constipation or diarrhea. Cimetidine has many drug interactions (inhibits cytochrome P450 enzymes).
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; supportive care
Nursing Considerations
Generally well tolerated. Famotidine is preferred over Cimetidine due to fewer drug interactions. May be used for on-demand acid relief.
Patient Teaching
Can be taken with or without food. Available over the counter for heartburn. Not as strong or long-lasting as Proton Pump Inhibitors for severe acid conditions. Inform provider of all medications if taking Cimetidine.
Antiemetics — Serotonin Antagonists Suffix: -setron
Ondansetron (Zofran), Granisetron, Palonosetron
▼
Uses
Nausea and vomiting from chemotherapy, surgery, radiation, or post-operative recovery
Mechanism of Action
Blocks serotonin (type 3, also written as 5-HT3) receptors in the gut and chemoreceptor trigger zone, suppressing the vomiting reflex
Side Effects
QT prolongation (cardiac risk — can cause arrhythmia), headache, constipation, dizziness
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; monitor electrocardiogram for QT prolongation; discontinue if arrhythmia occurs
Nursing Considerations
Obtain baseline electrocardiogram in at-risk patients. Use with caution in patients with prolonged QT or those on other QT-prolonging drugs. Monitor electrolytes (hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia worsen QT risk).
Patient Teaching
Report irregular heartbeat, dizziness, or fainting. Can be taken orally or intravenously. Works best when taken before nausea onset. Constipation is common — increase fluid and fiber intake.
Pain Medications
Opioid Analgesics
Morphine, Hydromorphone (Dilaudid), Oxycodone, Fentanyl, Codeine
▼
Uses
Moderate to severe pain, dyspnea in palliative care, acute coronary syndrome pain, anesthesia
Mechanism of Action
Binds to mu-opioid receptors in the central nervous system and peripheral tissues, altering pain perception and response
Side Effects
Respiratory depression (most dangerous), constipation, sedation, nausea, urinary retention, pruritus, tolerance and physical dependence with long-term use
Antidote / Reversal
Naloxone (Narcan) — reverses respiratory depression rapidly
Nursing Considerations
Monitor respiratory rate (hold if below 12 breaths/minute), sedation level, and oxygen saturation. Have Naloxone at bedside. Assess pain relief. Implement bowel program to prevent constipation.
Patient Teaching
Do not drive or operate machinery. Do not mix with alcohol or benzodiazepines. Take stool softeners to prevent constipation. Do not stop abruptly after long-term use — taper needed. Lock up medications securely.
Nonsteroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs
Ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin), Ketorolac (Toradol), Naproxen (Aleve), Indomethacin
▼
Uses
Mild to moderate pain, fever, inflammation, arthritis; Ketorolac is used for short-term acute pain in the hospital setting
Mechanism of Action
Inhibits cyclooxygenase enzymes (cyclooxygenase-1 and cyclooxygenase-2), reducing prostaglandin synthesis — decreasing pain, fever, and inflammation
Side Effects
Gastrointestinal bleeding and ulcers, acute kidney injury, platelet inhibition (increased bleeding risk), cardiovascular risk with long-term use, fluid retention
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; discontinue drug; treat gastrointestinal bleeding with proton pump inhibitors and supportive care
Nursing Considerations
Monitor kidney function and stool for blood. Use with caution in elderly, patients with kidney disease, heart failure, or history of gastrointestinal ulcers. Avoid in patients on blood thinners.
Patient Teaching
Take with food or milk to reduce stomach upset. Report black or tarry stools or blood in urine. Avoid in the last trimester of pregnancy. Limit alcohol. Do not exceed the recommended dose.
Acetaminophen (Nonopioid Analgesic/Antipyretic)
Acetaminophen (Tylenol, Paracetamol)
▼
Uses
Mild to moderate pain, fever reduction; preferred analgesic in patients who cannot take Nonsteroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs
Mechanism of Action
Inhibits prostaglandin synthesis centrally; mechanism not fully understood. Does NOT have significant anti-inflammatory effects at therapeutic doses.
Side Effects
Well tolerated at therapeutic doses. Major risk: Hepatotoxicity (liver failure) with overdose or chronic alcohol use. Max safe dose is 4,000 mg/day in healthy adults (lower with liver disease or alcohol use).
Antidote / Reversal
N-Acetylcysteine (Mucomyst) — most effective if given within 8–10 hours of overdose
Nursing Considerations
Check all medications for hidden acetaminophen (found in many combination products). Monitor liver function tests. Question patients about alcohol use. Do not exceed maximum daily dose.
Patient Teaching
Do not exceed 4,000 mg per day (3,000 mg if older adult or liver concerns). Check all over-the-counter cold and pain products for acetaminophen content to avoid doubling up. Avoid alcohol. Report yellow skin or eyes.
Antibiotics
Penicillins
Amoxicillin, Amoxicillin-Clavulanate (Augmentin), Piperacillin-Tazobactam (Zosyn), Ampicillin
▼
Uses
Bacterial infections including ear, respiratory, urinary tract, skin, and streptococcal infections; broad-spectrum options for hospital-acquired infections
Mechanism of Action
Inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding to penicillin-binding proteins, causing cell lysis and death
Side Effects
Allergic reactions (ranging from rash to anaphylaxis), diarrhea, gastrointestinal upset, Clostridium difficile colitis
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; treat anaphylaxis with epinephrine, diphenhydramine, and corticosteroids; discontinue drug
Nursing Considerations
Ask about penicillin allergy before administration. Approximately 1–10% of penicillin-allergic patients have cross-reactivity with cephalosporins. Monitor for rash, hives, or anaphylaxis during and after infusion.
Patient Teaching
Complete the full course even if feeling better. Report any rash, itching, or difficulty breathing immediately. Take Amoxicillin with or without food. Probiotics may help reduce diarrhea.
Cephalosporins
Cefazolin (Ancef) — first generation; Ceftriaxone (Rocephin) — third generation; Cephalexin (Keflex)
▼
Uses
Wide range of bacterial infections; Cefazolin is commonly used for surgical prophylaxis; Ceftriaxone for meningitis, pneumonia, gonorrhea
Mechanism of Action
Inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis (similar to Penicillins); later generations cover broader gram-negative organisms
Side Effects
Allergic reactions (less common than Penicillins), diarrhea, Clostridium difficile risk, false positive urine glucose test
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; treat allergic reactions with epinephrine and supportive care
Nursing Considerations
Ask about penicillin AND cephalosporin allergies — there is a small cross-reactivity risk. Administer Cefazolin before surgical incision (usually within 60 minutes). Monitor for diarrhea or Clostridium difficile symptoms.
Patient Teaching
Complete the full antibiotic course. Report rash, hives, or severe diarrhea. Refrigerate liquid formulations. Take oral forms with food if gastrointestinal upset occurs.
Macrolides
Azithromycin (Z-Pack), Erythromycin, Clarithromycin
▼
Uses
Atypical pneumonia (walking pneumonia), respiratory infections, skin infections, sexually transmitted infections; used in penicillin-allergic patients
Mechanism of Action
Inhibits bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the 50S ribosomal subunit, preventing bacterial growth
Side Effects
Gastrointestinal upset (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea), QT prolongation (cardiac arrhythmia risk), hearing loss with high doses, drug interactions (cytochrome P450 inhibitor)
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; monitor electrocardiogram and manage arrhythmias supportively; discontinue drug
Nursing Considerations
Obtain baseline electrocardiogram for at-risk patients. Monitor potassium and magnesium (low levels increase QT risk). Be aware of numerous drug interactions (especially Clarithromycin and Erythromycin).
Patient Teaching
Take Azithromycin on an empty stomach if tolerated; food reduces absorption. Complete the full course. Report irregular heartbeat or hearing changes. Avoid antacids within 2 hours of dose.
Fluoroquinolones Suffix: -floxacin
Ciprofloxacin (Cipro), Levofloxacin (Levaquin), Moxifloxacin (Avelox)
▼
Uses
Urinary tract infections, respiratory infections, gastrointestinal infections, anthrax exposure, certain sexually transmitted infections
Mechanism of Action
Inhibits bacterial deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) gyrase and topoisomerase IV enzymes, preventing DNA replication and repair
Side Effects
Tendon rupture (especially Achilles — BLACK BOX WARNING), QT prolongation, peripheral neuropathy, central nervous system effects (dizziness, insomnia, seizures), photosensitivity, Clostridium difficile
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; discontinue immediately with tendon pain or arrhythmia; supportive care
Nursing Considerations
Do NOT use in children or pregnant women (affects cartilage development). Monitor for tendon pain — stop drug immediately if it occurs. Avoid with antacids, iron, zinc, and dairy (reduce absorption). Monitor electrocardiogram for QT prolongation.
Patient Teaching
Stop immediately and report tendon pain, swelling, or inability to bear weight. Use sunscreen — causes sun sensitivity. Drink plenty of fluids. Do not take with antacids, dairy, or iron within 2–4 hours. Do not drive if dizzy.
Aminoglycosides
Gentamicin, Tobramycin, Amikacin
▼
Uses
Serious gram-negative bacterial infections (sepsis, hospital-acquired pneumonia, endocarditis — often in combination with other antibiotics)
Mechanism of Action
Inhibits bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the 30S ribosomal subunit; bactericidal
Side Effects
Nephrotoxicity (kidney damage — dose-dependent), ototoxicity (hearing loss and balance problems — can be permanent), neuromuscular blockade
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; discontinue drug; support renal function; hearing loss may be permanent
Nursing Considerations
Monitor drug levels — peak and trough levels (or area under the curve with extended interval dosing). Monitor creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, and urine output closely. Baseline audiometry recommended. Avoid concurrent nephrotoxic drugs.
Patient Teaching
Report ringing in the ears, dizziness, or changes in hearing. Stay well hydrated. Report decreased urine output or dark urine. Inform all providers of this medication due to drug interaction risks.
Vancomycin (Glycopeptide Antibiotic)
Vancomycin (Vancocin)
▼
Uses
Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus infections, serious gram-positive infections, Clostridium difficile colitis (oral form)
Mechanism of Action
Inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding to the terminal peptide chains, preventing cross-linking — different mechanism from beta-lactams
Side Effects
Nephrotoxicity (kidney damage), Red Man Syndrome (flushing, rash, hypotension — infusion-related, not a true allergy), ototoxicity
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; for Red Man Syndrome: slow infusion rate, premedicate with diphenhydramine; for nephrotoxicity: dose adjust and discontinue if severe
Nursing Considerations
Infuse slowly — at least 60 minutes per gram (to prevent Red Man Syndrome). Monitor vancomycin trough or area-under-the-curve levels. Monitor kidney function and urine output. Avoid other nephrotoxic drugs.
Patient Teaching
Report flushing, redness, or itching during infusion — this is treatable by slowing the drip. Report decreased urination or swelling. Report ringing in the ears. This antibiotic requires close monitoring — do not miss scheduled lab draws.
Neurological and Psychiatric Medications
Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors
Sertraline (Zoloft), Fluoxetine (Prozac), Escitalopram (Lexapro), Paroxetine (Paxil)
▼
Uses
Depression, anxiety disorders, obsessive-compulsive disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, panic disorder, premenstrual dysphoric disorder
Mechanism of Action
Inhibits reuptake of serotonin at the presynaptic neuron, increasing serotonin availability in the synaptic cleft
Side Effects
Sexual dysfunction, gastrointestinal upset (nausea, diarrhea), insomnia or sedation, headache, weight changes. Serotonin syndrome risk (especially when combined with other serotonergic drugs). Increased suicidal ideation risk in young adults initially.
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; for serotonin syndrome: discontinue drug, supportive care, Cyproheptadine (serotonin antagonist) for severe cases
Nursing Considerations
Monitor for suicidal ideation — especially during the first few weeks of treatment. Assess mood and behavior changes. Do not stop abruptly. Therapeutic effect takes 2–4 weeks. Screen for serotonin syndrome if on multiple serotonergic drugs.
Patient Teaching
Full effect may take 4–6 weeks — do not stop early because it seems ineffective. Do not stop abruptly (discontinuation syndrome). Report worsening mood or suicidal thoughts. Avoid alcohol and other serotonergic substances. Report agitation, rapid heartbeat, or high fever (serotonin syndrome signs).
Benzodiazepines Suffix: -pam / -lam
Lorazepam (Ativan), Diazepam (Valium), Midazolam (Versed), Alprazolam (Xanax)
▼
Uses
Anxiety, seizures, alcohol withdrawal, procedural sedation, muscle relaxation, insomnia
Mechanism of Action
Enhances the effect of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA — the brain's main inhibitory neurotransmitter) by binding to GABA-A receptors, producing sedation, anxiolysis, and anticonvulsant effects
Side Effects
Sedation, respiratory depression (especially with opioids), hypotension, anterograde amnesia, physical dependence and withdrawal, paradoxical agitation in elderly
Antidote / Reversal
Flumazenil (Romazicon) — reverses sedation; caution: short-acting, resedation can occur; can precipitate seizures in dependent patients
Nursing Considerations
Monitor respiratory rate and level of consciousness closely. NEVER combine with opioids without careful monitoring (synergistic respiratory depression). Have Flumazenil and resuscitation equipment available. Fall risk — use bed alarms and assist with ambulation.
Patient Teaching
Do not drive or operate machinery. Avoid alcohol (dangerous interaction). Do not stop abruptly — tapering required to prevent withdrawal seizures. Physical dependence can develop even with prescribed use.
Antiepileptic Drugs (Anticonvulsants)
Phenytoin (Dilantin), Levetiracetam (Keppra), Valproate (Depakote), Carbamazepine (Tegretol)
▼
Uses
Seizure disorders, epilepsy; some agents used for bipolar disorder, neuropathic pain, and migraine prevention
Mechanism of Action
Varies by drug: Phenytoin stabilizes sodium channels; Levetiracetam modulates synaptic vesicle protein; Valproate enhances gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and blocks sodium channels
Side Effects
Phenytoin: gingival hyperplasia (gum overgrowth), ataxia, nystagmus, hirsutism, teratogenicity, cardiac arrhythmias with rapid intravenous infusion. Levetiracetam: irritability, behavioral changes, minimal drug interactions.
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; manage toxicity with supportive care; discontinue offending drug
Nursing Considerations
Monitor therapeutic drug levels (especially Phenytoin — narrow therapeutic index). Phenytoin: administer intravenously slowly (max 50 mg/minute — risk of hypotension and cardiac arrhythmia). Do not administer in dextrose solutions. Monitor for signs of toxicity.
Patient Teaching
Never stop seizure medications abruptly. Take at the same time every day. Practice good dental hygiene if on Phenytoin (gum overgrowth). Report behavioral changes or suicidal thoughts. Avoid driving until seizure-free period per state law.
Women's Health Medications
Oxytocin
Oxytocin (Pitocin)
▼
Uses
Induction or augmentation of labor, prevention and treatment of postpartum hemorrhage
Mechanism of Action
Binds to oxytocin receptors in the uterus, stimulating rhythmic uterine contractions; also promotes milk let-down
Side Effects
Uterine hyperstimulation (most serious — can cause fetal distress), water intoxication (antidiuretic effect), hypotension, nausea, fetal heart rate abnormalities
Antidote / Reversal
No specific antidote; stop infusion immediately for hyperstimulation; reposition patient, give oxygen, notify provider
Nursing Considerations
Continuous fetal and uterine monitoring required. Titrate by protocol. Stop infusion if contractions last more than 90 seconds or occur more than 5 times in 10 minutes. Monitor intake and output for water retention.
Patient Teaching
Medication will be used to help start or strengthen contractions. Report pain level changes. The baby will be continuously monitored throughout the process.
Magnesium Sulfate
Magnesium Sulfate (intravenous infusion)
▼
Uses
Preeclampsia and eclampsia seizure prevention and treatment; preterm labor tocolysis; hypomagnesemia
Mechanism of Action
Decreases neuromuscular excitability by competing with calcium at motor nerve terminals, producing central nervous system depression and muscle relaxation
Side Effects
Flushing, diaphoresis, hypotension; Toxicity signs: loss of deep tendon reflexes (first sign), respiratory depression, cardiac arrest at very high levels
Antidote / Reversal
Calcium gluconate — administer 10 mL of 10% solution intravenously slowly; keep at bedside at all times
Nursing Considerations
Check deep tendon reflexes before every dose — if absent, hold drug and notify provider. Monitor respiratory rate (must be above 12 breaths/minute). Monitor urine output (must be above 25–30 mL/hour). Keep Calcium gluconate at bedside.
Patient Teaching
You may feel warm and flushed — this is expected. Report any difficulty breathing, extreme weakness, or feeling very drowsy. Nurses will check your reflexes frequently.
